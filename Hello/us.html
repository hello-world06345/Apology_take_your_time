Chapter IV

Us

I owe it to you — and to us — to be honest about what I did wrong, and what it taught me.

Where I Went Wrong

Taking my frustration out on you

When grief hit me, I didn't know what to do with the anger. Instead of sitting with it or asking for help, I let it spill onto you — the person who least deserved it. My tone was sharp. My words were careless. I treated you like you were the problem when you were the only good thing.

Using a harsh tone when you reached out

You'd call to check on me and I'd answer with coldness. You'd text something kind and I'd reply with one word. I made your love feel like an interruption when it was the only thing keeping me afloat.

Saying "we should take a break"

That sentence. I know what it did to you. I framed it as protecting you, but it was me running from the guilt of knowing I was hurting you. It made you feel disposable in the one place you should feel safest — with me.

Shutting you out instead of letting you in

I kept my grief locked inside and built walls you couldn't climb. You were right there, ready to hold me, and I acted like I had to do it alone. That wasn't strength. That was fear.

Making you doubt your place in my life

Through all of this, the worst thing I did was make you question whether you matter to me. You do. More than I've shown. More than my recent actions have said.

What I Learned

Pain is not a pass to hurt others

Going through something terrible doesn't give me the right to become someone terrible to the people I love. My grief is mine to carry — not mine to weaponize.

Vulnerability is not weakness

Saying "I'm not okay" to you is not losing. It's the bravest thing I can do in a relationship. You've been waiting for me to let you in — I need to actually do it.

Love requires emotional responsibility

Loving you means managing my emotions before they reach you as damage. It means pausing when I'm frustrated, and choosing my words instead of throwing them.

You are not my therapist — you are my partner

I've been leaning on you in ways that aren't fair. You can support me, but I need to also do the work — journal, talk to someone, process my grief in healthy ways so I don't make you carry it all.

Staying is a choice that deserves respect

Every day you stay is a gift. I need to treat it that way — not by being perfect, but by being consistent, honest, and gentle with the person who chose me even at my worst.

I can't undo what I've done. But I can decide what happens next.
